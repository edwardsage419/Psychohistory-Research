## Source and new question
Rabiner (1989), *A Tutorial on Hidden Markov Models and Selected Applications in Speech Recognition*, DOI https://doi.org/10.1109/5.18626. Original R300 demonstrated exact known-parameter filtering and an IID hidden-state negative control. S96 asks how one-step forecasts deteriorate if the analyst assumes the wrong hidden-state transition persistence or emission reliability. It does not estimate HMM parameters.

## Frozen grid and full exact source
Two true worlds: hidden stay 1/2 (IID) and 3/4 (persistent); true emission match 3/4. Each has all 16 four-bit observed histories and exact probability weights. Nine candidate models per world use stay in {1/4,1/2,3/4} and emission match in {1/2,3/4,1}. Thus 288 source rows, 18 aggregate candidate risks, 32 distinct true prefix events. Every candidate forecast is scored against a new fifth observation under the true world, with exact expected Brier risk and oracle excess. Full 288-row CSV and all 18 groups are archived; no Monte Carlo approximation or human data.

For true prefix probability w, true next event probability p, candidate prediction f, exact conditional Brier is p(1-f)^2+(1-p)f^2. The expected excess above the true oracle is sum_y w(y)(f(y)-p(y))^2.

Under a true IID latent process, an assumed persistent HMM with informative emissions invents forecast dependence and increases Brier risk. A deterministic sensor assumption (emission match1) combined with wrong persistence can worsen risk further. Under true persistence, correct filtering reduces risk by 1962931/430168816 ≈0.004563 compared with the 1/2 baseline; wrong antipersistent or perfect-sensor assumptions can erase or reverse this gain.

Negative controls: when candidate stay=1/2, or emission match=1/2, its next observation forecast is always 1/2 regardless of the observed history. The true IID world has next event probability exactly 1/2 for every prefix. All oracle excess values are nonnegative. The threshold and numerical risk sizes depend on this finite fixed grid and cannot be generalized to unknown social systems.

## Verified historical observation
The paper reports approximately 9,185 Harvard employees enrolled across eight health plans in 1986. In 1980, 62% were in BCBS, 31% HUGHP and 6% HCHP. By 1986 the shares were 30.4% BCBS, 37.3% HUGHP and 13.2% HCHP; several new plans entered over this period. These are reported aggregate shares from the authors, not individual transition probabilities. The authors compare older and newer enrollees, stratifying by age because tenure and age composition differ. A cited earlier study reports only about 3% switching annually, but this number is secondary evidence within the paper and was not independently checked.

## Inference boundary and competing explanations
An older enrollee retaining a plan while a newer enrollee chooses another is consistent with status-quo inertia. It does not alone identify an individual cognitive bias. Plan quality, coverage, provider continuity, health needs, employer subsidies, available choices by year, age, selection into continued employment and genuine switching costs may differ. Age stratification alone cannot guarantee exchangeability. The aggregate 1980 and 1986 percentages must not be interpreted as a within-person causal effect. Historical US insurance arrangements must not be copied into current jurisdictions as plan-specific advice.

## Distinct candidate rule
For recurring real-world choices, ask: “Has the current option remained preferable under today's actual terms, or are you keeping it because it was previously selected? Which verifiable changes in price, coverage, access, obligations or transition costs occurred since the last review?” Record the effective date, current versus new-option eligibility, actual switching cost and whether the comparison is observational. Present an option to preserve the status quo for legitimate reasons. No automatic diagnosis of bias or preference modification.

## Six synthetic failure examples for independent acceptance
1. Treat 1980 and 1986 aggregate plan shares as if 31.6% of the same individuals switched away from BCBS: FAIL.
2. Compare older and new enrollees without noting differing age or eligibility: FAIL.
3. Treat newly introduced plans as available in 1980: FAIL.
4. Ignore verified provider continuity or cancellation costs and force a switch: FAIL.
5. Present historical Harvard percentages as current personal insurance probabilities: FAIL.
6. Change the user's recorded value weights because a framing-sensitive group pattern exists: FAIL.

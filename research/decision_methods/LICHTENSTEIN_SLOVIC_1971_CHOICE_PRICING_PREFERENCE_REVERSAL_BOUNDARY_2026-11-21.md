# Choice versus pricing preference reversal: Lichtenstein and Slovic (1971)

Actual read: original abstract, introduction, Experiment I methods/results, Experiment II methods/results and Table 1, Figure 1; remaining article not comprehensively audited.
Three experiments compare direct paired choices and monetary buying/selling valuations of gambles. Experiment I had 173 male undergraduates using hypothetical gambles. Experiment II had 74 college students using hypothetical gambles. A high probability gamble could be selected while a higher prize gamble received a higher bid. In Experiment I 127 of 173 (73%) participants reversed on every eligible P-chosen pair, a conditional statistic and not a personal probability. In Experiment II loss amount differences related to choices, whereas prize differences related to bids. The authors propose response-mode-dependent information processing.
Original illustration: P: 0.99 win $4, 0.01 lose $1, expected value $3.95. Dollar gamble: 0.33 win $16, 0.67 lose $2, expected value $3.94. The values differ by $0.01; do not call them exactly equal.
Candidate question: “You chose A directly but assigned B a higher monetary value or score. Is that because the questions emphasize different aspects, affordability or hard constraints, new information, or an actual preference change?” Preserve both original responses, exact wording, elicitation mode, ownership/reference point, units and time. Do not overwrite either response or automatically infer a stable single ranking.
Synthetic acceptance failures:
1. Direct choice A, pricing B>A, system overwrites A with B.
2. Buying and selling bids treated as interchangeable without ownership/reference context.
3. Different currencies, time windows or outcome definitions silently ranked.
4. Hard budget constraint reclassified as bias and overridden.
5. Experiment I's 73% conditional participant fraction assigned as this user's reversal probability.
6. Original illustration described as exactly equal expected values.
Competing explanations: anchoring to prize, response-mode attention, buying/selling reference points, income effects, noisy preferences, changed preferences, task learning and hypothetical incentives. Gambling findings do not provide numerical correction factors for career or relocation decisions. Discrepancy triggers clarification, not automatic irrationality labeling.
Distinct from longitudinal preference snapshots (Sharot 2012), complementary framing (McNeil 1982) and ambiguity inference (Ellsberg 1961): same options, different elicitation method. Full repository semantic deduplication partial.

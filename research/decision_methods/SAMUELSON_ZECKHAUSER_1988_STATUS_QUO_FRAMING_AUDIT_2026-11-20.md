# Status quo framing audit for personal decisions (2026-11-20)

Status: research and candidate acceptance preparation only. No individual calibration, deployment, independent replication or scientific acceptance.

## Original source and inspected scope
William Samuelson and Richard Zeckhauser (1988), *Status Quo Bias in Decision Making*, Journal of Risk and Uncertainty 1(1), 7–59, DOI 10.1007/BF00055564. Harvard-hosted publisher scan: https://scholar.harvard.edu/sites/scholar.harvard.edu/files/rzeckhauser/files/status_quo_bias_in_decision_making.pdf . Inspected original journal pp. 7–15, especially pp. 8, 12–15, experimental design, and Table 1a. The original paper also covers field studies and competing explanations; those sections were not fully audited here.

## Evidence and calculations
The authors compare neutral framing with assigning an option the current/default position while holding the option set fixed. The questionnaire involved 486 student participants, hypothetical decisions and no monetary incentives (p. 14). In Table 1a, the East Coast choice in the two-option relocation question is 16/20 = 80% when status quo, 23/31 = 74.19% under neutral framing, and 8/38 = 21.05% as a non-status-quo alternative. Status-quo versus non-status-quo difference = 58.95 percentage points. These are distinct participant groups, not paired individual changes; denominators and rounding are preserved. The authors report a significance value of .001 for the corresponding question, not independently recomputed here. These data are evidence of framing sensitivity in this experimental setting, not a quantified personal effect size.

## Decision capability and candidate rule
For a choice involving staying with a current job, investment, location, subscription, relationship arrangement or policy, record: current option, alternative outcomes, transition cost, reversibility, legal entitlements, uncertainty and the user's reasons for preferring continuity. Present a second comparison with neutral option labels and equivalent end states, while retaining real switching costs and obligations. Ask: “If neither option were your current arrangement, and all genuine switching costs and commitments were still included, would you rank them the same way?” If ranking changes, flag framing sensitivity for user review, without replacing the user's weights or forcing a switch.

## Synthetic acceptance cases
Case A: two plans have identical final outcomes, costs and constraints. Merely marking Plan A as “currently selected” must not alter the system's numerical objective scores. User-reported subjective preference may change and must be retained separately.
Case B: current plan has a verified 30-unit cancellation fee; the alternative has a 20-unit gain before switching. The system must preserve the real fee and may rationally retain the status quo. Erasing this fee in a purported neutral comparison is a failure.
Case C: user chooses to keep an existing plan for continuity, identity or relational commitments. The system must allow those user-confirmed values as legitimate objectives.

## Assumptions, competing explanations and limits
Competing explanations: genuine transaction and learning costs, uncertainty, rational option value of waiting, commitment/identity, regret avoidance, loss aversion, habit and presentation order. The laboratory manipulation cannot uniquely identify any mechanism for an individual. The source's field evidence needs separate causal scrutiny. No personal probability or coefficient should be inferred from the observed group percentages.

## Primary-source observations and evidentiary classification

1. p.194: the "Last National Bank" and named president are introduced as a narrative example. The subsequent reference to the bank as a sociological parable makes it inappropriate to code that named institution's collapse as an independently documented 1932 historical bank event. The parable illustrates a possible mechanism, not an identified empirical effect.
2. pp.208–209: Merton offers three historical period averages of US bank suspensions: 635 annually in the 1920s; 2,276 annually in four years around the 1929 crash; 28 annually over twelve years after FDIC establishment and other banking laws. These are numbers *reported by Merton* and checked against the scanned printed page, not independently reconstructed from US bank-level historical records. The phrase "four years immediately before and after The Crash" leaves exact endpoints ambiguous in this excerpt. The three windows also differ in duration and macroeconomic conditions.
3. p.209: the proposed institutional explanation explicitly combines FDIC with other banking legislation, while the comparison spans depression, recovery, recession and expansion. Therefore even accepting all three reported averages as accurate cannot identify a causal effect of deposit insurance alone. Alternative explanations include changed banking regulation, bank population, monetary conditions, closure/reporting definitions, selection and time trends. Need official contemporaneous suspension series, bank population denominators, policy dates and suitable counterfactual comparisons.
4. pp.209–210: Merton discusses a biracial Pittsburgh housing project and reports descriptive within-project fractions and expectations. The paper states the full Lavanburg study is not reported there. This is an author-described observational case; sample sizes, selection, questionnaire wording, baseline comparators and assignment mechanism are not provided in the inspected pages. Cannot estimate the causal effect of the project's administration or transport the proportions to other populations.
5. p.210: the author argues institutional change can interrupt a feedback loop. This is a competing mechanism against a fixed-trait or inevitability account. The text provides examples and qualitative reasoning; it does not supply a modern identified causal design or calibrated forecast.

## Independent arithmetic and identification checks

The reported post-law annual average 28 is 607 lower than 635 and 2,248 lower than 2,276. Ratios 28/635 = 0.04409 and 28/2276 = 0.01230. These are comparisons of published *period averages*, not effect sizes. No uncertainty intervals or baseline bank counts are provided in the inspected article. A counterexample to causal attribution: construct two historical worlds with identical observed suspension averages and policy timing. In world A, deposit insurance changes depositor behavior; in world B, other simultaneous banking reforms and macroeconomic stabilization explain the fall while deposit insurance has zero incremental effect. Observations cited in the article cannot distinguish them.

## Competing explanations, layers, limits

Individual: depositors' beliefs, housing residents' expectations. Group: runs and intergroup interaction. Institutional: insurance, banking law, housing governance. Historical: heterogeneous crisis and post-crisis regimes. Competing explanations: underlying bank solvency, macro conditions, concurrent policy packages, compositional changes and observational selection. A proposed falsification design would fix historical bank closure definitions and policy dates, compare exposed and plausibly less-exposed institutions or jurisdictions while checking pretrends and concurrent reforms; feasibility and identifying assumptions remain unverified.

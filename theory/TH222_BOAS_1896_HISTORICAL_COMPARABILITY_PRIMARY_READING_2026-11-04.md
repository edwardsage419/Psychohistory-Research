## Original claims, with page boundaries
1. pp. 902–904: identical or similar observed customs need not share a developmental route. Boas contrasts clan formation through association versus subdivision, and geometric motifs produced by distinct processes. The observed outcome alone cannot identify the history.
2. pp. 904–905: cross-cultural comparisons require prior evidence of causal and historical comparability; the same observable outcome can result from distinct processes. His target is a universal single-line evolution inferred from resemblance.
3. pp. 905–907: historical inquiry traces a practice within its local cultural context, geographical distribution, psychological conditions, and exchange history before generalizing. Environmental determinism and diffusion are rival explanations; he does not categorically reject comparative general laws.
4. pp. 907–908: when historical connection is established, two observations are not independent evidence. This is an explicit dependence warning distinct from the modern statistical formalization. The essay also allows cautious cross-case comparisons after historically grounded investigation.

## Competing accounts and limits
The criticized universal developmental-route account presumes identical observed outcomes imply the same causes. A pure geographical determinist account presumes similar environment is sufficient. Diffusion/shared ancestry can create correlated cultural traits. Independent innovation can yield similar traits without shared ancestry. These are competing explanations requiring process, chronology, geographic contact and provenance evidence.
Boas's own examples are author-reported historical/ethnographic assertions, not independently reproduced case datasets here. Some assertions about geographic continuity as evidence for common origins require modern counterchecking, and the 1896 text reflects period terminology. No claim of historical certainty is adopted.

## Constructive identifiability counterexample (author-independent derivation)
Let Y=1 denote an observed shared cultural trait in communities A and B. World W1: a common ancestor transmits Y to both. World W2: independent innovations each produce Y. Both imply observed (Y_A,Y_B)=(1,1), while their causal histories differ. Thus the observation map from causal histories to Y is many-to-one. To distinguish worlds, additional observations such as chronology, genealogical provenance, transmission contact and intermediate variants are necessary; these may remain missing or selected. This is a logical counterexample, not an empirical demonstration or a numerical test.

## Measurement, causality, and falsifiability
Observable candidate variables: dated trait descriptions, documentary provenance, contact pathways, independently dated intermediate variants, ecological context, recording/selection processes. A falsifiable historical account must predict discriminating traces not used to invent its explanation, with rival histories specified before adjudication. Nonobservation of contact is not proof of independent invention, and shared contact does not prove a particular transmission route. Publication and archival survival bias remain possible. Historical observational evidence and logical counterexample are distinct from randomized experiments or out-of-sample historical prediction.

## Research question
How does a locally accepted explanation of an adverse event coexist with empirical knowledge of physical mechanisms, and how do social rules determine which attribution is actionable?

## Primary-source propositions
1. Granary case (printed pp. 22–23): termite damage explains structural collapse and people seeking shade explains their presence; witchcraft supplies an explanation of the harmful intersection at a particular time. The author explicitly rejects the caricature that Azande attribute every physical mechanism to witchcraft.
2. The 'second spear' analogy (pp. 25–26) expresses simultaneous attribution to ordinary physical causes and witchcraft, with different causal roles. This is a description of indigenous causal discourse, not empirical proof of mystical causation.
3. Attribution is institutionally selective (pp. 26–30): lying, theft, incompetence, taboo violations, and royal executions can make witchcraft an inadmissible or socially inappropriate account. The speaker's social position and the seriousness of the outcome can also alter the explanation given.
4. The author says (pp. 23–24) that his generalizations were inferred from situated accounts rather than from a formal causal doctrine elicited from respondents. His analytical reconstruction must be kept separate from informants' verbatim claims.

## Competing explanations and falsification possibilities
The same reported attribution pattern might arise from socially enforced accountability, retrospective narrative selection, differential access to evidence, or pragmatic conversational relevance. These explanations can coexist or compete. Distinguishing them would require independently recorded event mechanisms, speaker/role, timing, audience, sanctions, and responses to controlled information changes. The text provides qualitative historical ethnography, not random assignment, a population sample frame, or predictive validation.

## Scope and limitations
Individual: situational causal judgments. Group: shared explanation conventions. Institution: rules governing responsibility, permissible accusation and sanction. Historical: a situated colonial-era ethnography; generalization to contemporary Azande or other cultures UNKNOWN. Observer translation and selection, omitted cases, historical power relations, and 1976 editorial abridgement are limitations. No model fitting, artificial tests, or personal data used.

## Research contribution and boundaries
This primary-text reading supplies a counterexample to any proposed universal rule that supernatural attribution must displace empirical knowledge of proximate causes. It also motivates keeping observed physical mechanism, narrated ultimate cause, and socially actionable responsibility as distinct variables in future research. This is a conceptual counterexample, not a validated cross-cultural causal model. No project-wide synthesis or new R acceptance claimed.

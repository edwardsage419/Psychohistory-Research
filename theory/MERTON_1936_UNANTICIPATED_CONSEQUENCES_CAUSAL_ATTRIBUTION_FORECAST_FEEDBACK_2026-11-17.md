## Primary text observations and theoretical claims

1. pp. 894–896: The paper focuses mainly on isolated purposive actions; it explicitly leaves integrated systems of action largely outside scope. An unforeseen consequence need not be harmful. Merton distinguishes intended results, evaluative desirability and causal consequences. The rigorous causal criterion on p. 895 asks whether an outcome would have occurred without the action. This is a conceptual counterfactual criterion, not an identified statistical estimator.
2. pp. 896–898: Purposive action is not assumed to be objectively rational. Successful outcomes can follow poor ex ante choices and rational choices can fail. Two empirical obstacles are causal attribution and reconstructing actors' actual purposes, including ex post rationalization. Organized action offers more explicit goal records, not necessarily truthful ones.
3. pp. 898–901: Incomplete knowledge and heterogeneous situations prevent certain prediction of individual social outcomes. Merton explicitly rejects the tautological explanation that enough knowledge would have guaranteed prediction. Acquiring further knowledge has time and resource costs. The text offers no calibrated forecasting formula.
4. pp. 901–903: Error, habitual transfer of previously successful routines to changed environments, immediate-interest dominance and basic-value commitments are distinct candidate mechanisms. Pursuing one value can affect other values, with feedback changing the original value system. These are analytical hypotheses and illustrative arguments, not independent historical tests here.
5. pp. 903–904: Public social forecasts can change subsequent behavior and thus alter the predicted trajectory. Merton gives an interpretation involving Marxist predictions and labor organization; the paper does not identify a historical counterfactual effect of the publication. The forecast feedback mechanism is a competing explanation requiring independent evidence.

## Competing explanations and discriminating evidence

An apparent policy failure could arise from inadequate prior information, erroneous extrapolation, narrow immediate objectives, conflicting values, implementation error, external shocks, or behavioral responses to public forecasts. These mechanisms need not be mutually exclusive. A historical study should record the policy's contemporaneous objectives and forecasts, information available at decision time, dissemination timing, relevant exposures, and comparison cases; retrospective actor statements alone are insufficient.

For forecast-feedback claims, compare outcomes under different forecast exposure, accounting for selection into exposure and common contemporaneous shocks. The counterfactual outcome without publication remains unobserved. An ex post mismatch between forecast and outcome cannot alone establish self-defeating prophecy.

## Independent constructed counterexample (logical, no empirical validation)

Let a public prediction state that event E will occur absent an intervention. Agents who hear it take a preventive action, and E does not occur. Two data-generating stories fit the same observed sequence: (A) prevention causally averts E; (B) E would never have occurred, and prevention has no effect. Observing prediction, prevention, and nonoccurrence alone cannot identify the feedback effect. This is an identification counterexample constructed in this research note, not a claim of Merton's original formal proof.

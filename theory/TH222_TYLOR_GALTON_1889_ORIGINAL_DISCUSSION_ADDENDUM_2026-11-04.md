## Primary evidence and correction
1. Tylor pp. 245–247: reports tabulating marriage and descent customs for approximately 350 peoples, with ceremonial avoidance by husband and wife's relatives observed 14 times among 65 wife-side residence cases versus 9 expected; 9 among 141 husband-side residence cases versus 18 expected. These are author-reported historical tabulations, with source and sampling quality unverified here. Tylor p. 248 expressly distinguishes a possible causal connection from a speculative causal reason.
2. Galton's reported remarks, printed p. 270: asks for information on the degree of independence among compared peoples, warns that customs inherited from a common source create duplicate observations, recommends weighting observations and mapping geographical ranges. This is now directly verified in the original printed discussion, resolving the prior bibliographic uncertainty about where the historical independence objection occurs.
3. Tylor's reply, printed p. 272: acknowledges the common-source duplication problem, proposes using sufficiently marked differences to classify separate cases, and suggests historical correspondences may approximately offset across the world. The cancellation is asserted, not demonstrated by a sampling design or numerical proof in the inspected pages.
4. The original dispute therefore separates two questions: whether observed traits are associated and whether those associations represent independently replicated evidence of a general process. The first does not resolve the second. Tylor's conditional causal caution in p. 248 should be retained alongside Galton's objection; presenting Tylor as claiming every association proves causation would misread his text.

## Constructive counterexample and scope
Consider n observed societies each with a matching pair of traits. World A: all n inherit both traits from one common historical source. World B: n genuinely independent processes generate the same pairs. The observed n rows are identical, but the count of independent generative events differs. This is a logical construction only. Spatial proximity and lineage evidence can help discriminate histories but do not alone establish the effective number of independent observations. Galton's proposed weighting has no unique numeric formula in the printed remarks. Tylor's approximate global cancellation is not guaranteed by symmetric geographical coverage.

## Competition and limits
Boas (1896) emphasizes reconstructing local historical processes before broad comparisons; Galton's objection is explicitly about dependent observations in a statistical comparison, and Tylor offers an alternative pragmatic response. Raoul Naroll (1961), “Two Solutions to Galton's Problem,” *Philosophy of Science* 28(1):15–39, DOI https://doi.org/10.1086/287778 , was read only at publisher abstract level this run. Its sifting and cluster tests remain unverified in full text; no claim that they solve the identification problem in all circumstances.
